Every dated event in Chapter 7, in order

Four trade networks and what moved along them besides goods: religions, technologies, and diseases. The book's Landmarks box starts at 1 CE and the chapter text reaches back to the Han–Roman Silk Roads, so the timeline starts before 600 CE.

Silk Roads

Overland Eurasia. Luxury goods for elites; Buddhism, Nestorian Christianity, Manichaeism; smallpox, measles, plague.

Sea Roads

Indian Ocean. Bulk goods on monsoon winds; Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism; Srivijaya, Swahili coast, Malacca.

Sand Roads

Trans-Saharan. Gold, salt, slaves, horses; Islam and Arabic literacy; Ghana, Mali, Songhay.

American network

Western Hemisphere. Maize, turquoise, obsidian, feathers, shells; regional, not hemispheric, exchange.

Sequences by network

Quizzes on this chapter usually test order within one network. Learn these four chains.

Silk Roads: three booms, each ended by disruption

  1. 200 BCE–200 CE — Han China + Roman Empire. Ends with Han collapse and 2nd–3rd century epidemics.
  2. 7th–8th centuries — Tang + Abbasid + Byzantine. Ends with Tang decline and Abbasid fragmentation.
  3. 1200–1400 — Mongol Empire revitalizes the routes. Ends with the Black Death (enters Europe 1347) and Mongol collapse.

Religion chain: Buddhism to Central Asian cities and northern China (200 BCE–300 CE) → nomadic rulers (300s) → Dunhuang caves (400–1400). Technology: silk-making leaves China 500–600. Disease chain: Athens 430 BCE → smallpox/measles 2nd–3rd c. → Justinian plague 534–750 → Black Death 1347.

Sand Roads: camel, then three empires

  1. 1–300 — Camel introduced to North Africa and the Sahara.
  2. 300–400 — Trans-Saharan trade begins.
  3. ca. 500–1600 — West African civilization flourishes: Ghana (c. 700–1200) first.
  4. c. 1000 — West African rulers (Gao, Takrur) begin converting to Islam.
  5. c. 1235–1400s — Mali: Sundiata founds; Mansa Musa's hajj 1324; Ibn Battuta visits 1354.
  6. c. 1460s–1591 — Songhay.

American network: north to south

  1. ca. 850–1250 — Chaco culture (New Mexico) trades with Mesoamerica.
  2. ca. 900–1250 — Cahokia at the hub of a North American network.
  3. ca. 1000–1100 — Sweet potatoes spread from South America to Pacific Oceania.
  4. 1400–1500 — Aztec and Inca empires facilitate exchange.

The textbook's point: exchange stayed regional because there were no pack animals north of the Andes, no long-distance sailing, and no empire linking Mesoamerica to the Andes.
